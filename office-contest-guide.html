1. The timeline

Work backwards from contest day, which is usually Friday, October 30. Three checkpoints are all you need.

  • Three weeks out: announce the contest, the date, and the categories. Give people the costume quiz link (office-friendly costume ideas help too) so nobody can say they had no idea what to wear.
  • One week out: send the reminder with the voting plan - where ballots go, when the parade happens, when winners are announced. Half your turnout comes from this message.
  • Contest day: run the parade in the morning, collect ballots by mid-afternoon, announce winners at the all-hands or in the main channel. Done.

2. Pick four or five categories

Categories are the contest. Pick ones people can win without spending money, and every entry feels like it has a shot.

Funniest

The easiest category to enter and the most fun to judge. The Error 404 costume - a white t-shirt, markers, tape - wins this regularly because it is a joke the whole office gets.

Most Creative

For the builder. The person who turned a cardboard box into something unrecognizable takes this home.

Best DIY

The budget category. Under $10, built at home, and the materials list is part of the judging. This is where the closet-built costumes shine.

Best Duo or Team

Pairs and groups: Bacon and Eggs, Salt and Pepper, the Tetris Duo. Team entries pull in the people who would never dress up alone.

Most Desk-Friendly

The work-appropriate category: readable on a video call, comfortable in a chair, no fake blood, nothing that beeps during standup. Coffee Cup territory.

Scariest, lightly

Keep it light for the office: spooky, not gory. A glow skeleton beats anything with fake blood, which is banned at most desks anyway.

Rule of thumb: every category needs at least three real contenders or the win feels hollow. If turnout looks small, shrink to three categories.

3. Rules that keep it fair

Post the rules with the announcement. Five lines is enough, and it heads off every argument.

  • One ballot per person. No ballot-stuffing for your desk neighbor.
  • No voting for yourself. The most argued rule, so state it up front.
  • Work-appropriate only. If it would not survive an HR glance, it does not enter.
  • Teams enter team categories. A duo does not also sweep the solo categories.
  • Tally is anonymous. You announce the winners, not the vote counts. Close counts stay private.

One more call for small teams: under about 15 people, use a three-person judging panel instead of a popular vote. Popular votes in small offices turn into popularity contests. A panel of two managers and one neutral party is harder to argue with.

4. Voting that fits your office

Match the method to the room. All three work.

The ballot box

Print ballots, drop a box by the kitchen, collect by 3pm. Lowest tech, highest participation, because it takes ten seconds. The free contest tool prints ballots with your categories filled in.

The Slack or Teams vote

Post each contender as a photo with a number, then a 24-hour emoji window: react to vote. Great for hybrid teams because remote people vote on equal footing.

The show of hands

Parade at the all-hands, cheer-meter for each entry. Loud, fast, and slightly unfair to quiet costumes - use it for the fun categories, not the judged ones.

5. Prizes people actually want

The prize budget for an office contest is under $25, and honestly the trophy is the thing. What wins:

  • Leave early on a Friday. Costs nothing, valued above everything.
  • A coffee card. $10, universally correct.
  • The traveling trophy. A plastic crown or a printed certificate that lives on the winner's desk until next Halloween. The contest tool prints winner sheets; the certificate is the part people photograph.
  • Lunch with the boss. Cheap for the company, weirdly coveted.

Skip: anything over $25 (it makes the runners-up resentful), gag prizes that punch down, and prizes for "best" anything that reward spending money.

7. Including remote workers

Remote people get skipped by office contests more than anything else. The fix is a photo parade, and it takes ten minutes.

  • Photo thread: remote workers post a costume photo in a shared thread by the same 3pm deadline.
  • 24-hour voting window: emoji or form voting stays open a full day so every timezone gets a turn.
  • Announce on video: read the winners on a call, not just in chat, so remote people are in the room for it.
  • Ship the prize: a coffee card by email takes two minutes. Remote winners who get nothing tell the story for a year.

8. The people who skip dressing up

Every office has them, and the contest is better when they are not pressured. Two things actually move the needle:

  • Never make it mandatory. Mandatory fun is not fun, and it punishes tight budgets and real objections. Voluntary contests get higher turnout than forced ones.
  • Give them the easy on-ramp. The biggest excuse is not having an idea. A 2-minute quiz that builds a costume from what is already at home kills that excuse in one link.

FAQ

What are good categories for an office costume contest?

Pick four or five: Funniest, Most Creative, Best DIY, Best Duo or Team, Most Desk-Friendly, and a light Scariest. Fewer categories mean every vote counts.

How do you vote in an office costume contest fairly?

One ballot per person, no voting for yourself, anonymous tally. For small teams, a three-person judging panel beats a popular vote.

Do you need prizes for an office costume contest?

No, but they help turnout. Leave early on a Friday, a coffee card, and a traveling trophy cover it. Keep the budget under $25.

What if nobody at the office wants to dress up?

Keep it opt-in and give people an easy on-ramp. A 2-minute costume quiz that builds a costume from things at home removes the biggest excuse, which is not having an idea.

How do you include remote workers in a costume contest?

Photo parade in a shared thread, a 24-hour voting window for every timezone, winners announced on a video call, and the prize shipped by email.

Should an office costume contest be mandatory?

No. Voluntary contests with good categories get higher turnout than forced ones, and mandatory dress-up punishes tight budgets and real objections.

How many contest categories is too many?

Six is the ceiling and four or five is the sweet spot. Every category needs at least three real contenders or the win feels hollow.

What is the best way to announce contest winners?

At the all-hands or in the main team channel, one line per winner saying what they won and why. Use the winners template above and paste it as-is.
